Depth of Field & Diffraction

Near/far limits, hyperfocal distance — plus the diffraction limit most calculators ignore.

Full Frame 36 × 24change
Shared camera profile — set it once, every tool follows.
Formula H = f² / (N · c) + f · Dn = s(H − f) / (H + s − 2f) · Df = s(H − f) / (H − s)
Airy disk ⌀ = 2.44 · λ · N (λ = 550 nm) · Total blur = √(defocus² + diffraction²)

Depth of field is not a property of the lens — it is a decision about how much blur you are willing to call sharp. That tolerance is the circle of confusion (CoC): the largest out-of-focus disk that still reads as a point at your intended viewing size. Every depth-of-field number you have ever seen is downstream of that single assumption, which is why two calculators can disagree by a factor of two on the same lens.

Stoplume makes the assumption explicit and lets you change it. Pick the classic print criterion (diagonal ÷ 1442, which gives the familiar 0.030 mm on full frame), the stricter Zeiss criterion (÷ 1730), a critical 100 % pixel-peeping criterion (÷ 3000), or a genuinely resolution-aware criterion of two pixel pitches — the point at which the sensor itself can no longer record the difference.

The second half of the story is diffraction, which almost every free depth-of-field calculator leaves out. Stopping down shrinks the defocus blur but grows the Airy disk, so beyond a certain aperture your extra depth of field is paid for with a globally softer frame. Stoplume computes the Airy disk diameter, the diffraction-limited aperture for your chosen CoC, compares the Airy disk against your actual pixel pitch, and combines defocus and diffraction in quadrature to report the aperture where total blur is smallest for your subject.

Frequently asked

Why does your depth of field differ from another calculator?

Almost certainly the circle of confusion. A calculator hard-coded to 0.030 mm on full frame will report roughly 1.7× more depth of field than one using the Zeiss 0.019 mm-equivalent criterion. Stoplume shows the CoC it used in millimetres so you can reproduce or challenge the result.

What is the diffraction-limited aperture (DLA)?

It is the f-number at which the Airy disk grows to the same diameter as your circle of confusion. Past it, diffraction is the dominant blur and stopping down further trades overall sharpness for depth. DLA scales with sensor size, so a Micro Four Thirds body hits it two stops earlier than full frame.

Should I always focus at the hyperfocal distance for landscapes?

Only when you genuinely need detail at the near limit. Hyperfocal focusing puts infinity at the very edge of acceptable sharpness, so distant mountains will be exactly as soft as your CoC allows. If the far subject matters more, focus further out and accept a nearer limit further away.

Exposure Triangle & EV Solver

Solve any missing setting, convert to EV, and list every equivalent exposure.

Type a shutter speed as 1/250, 0.5 or 30s.
Formula EV100 = log₂(N² / t) · EVISO = EV100 + log₂(ISO / 100)
Every ±1 EV is one stop: halve or double the light.

Exposure value collapses aperture and shutter into a single number, so that all the combinations that admit the same amount of light land on the same EV. That is the whole trick behind the exposure triangle: f/8 at 1/250 s, f/5.6 at 1/500 s and f/11 at 1/125 s are one exposure wearing three different costumes — identical brightness, completely different depth of field and motion rendering.

This solver works in both directions. Give it any three of aperture, shutter, ISO and scene EV and it returns the fourth, applies your exposure compensation in thirds of a stop, snaps the answer to the nearest standard setting, and prints the full ladder of equivalent exposures so you can pick the trade-off you actually want.

The reference scene list uses the classical EV values photographers have used since hand-held meters: Sunny 16 is EV 15 at ISO 100, an overcast day is EV 12–13, a moonless Milky Way sky sits near EV −6. Those are starting points for a meterless estimate, not a substitute for metering the scene in front of you.

Frequently asked

Does changing ISO change the exposure?

It changes the brightness of the file, not the amount of light the lens delivered. EV as used here describes scene luminance at ISO 100; raising ISO lets you use a smaller aperture or faster shutter for the same output brightness, at the cost of noise.

Why does my camera show 1/8000 but the calculator says 1/6400?

Standard shutter values are rounded powers of two (1/8000 is really 1/8192). Stoplume prints the exact computed time and then the nearest full, half and third stop setting your camera can actually dial in.

Is Sunny 16 still accurate with digital cameras?

Yes — it describes the light, not the medium. On a clear day with the sun over your shoulder, f/16 with shutter ≈ 1/ISO lands within about half a stop, which is well inside raw headroom.

Crop Factor & Full-Frame Equivalence

Equivalent focal length, equivalent aperture and equivalent ISO between any two formats.

Full Frame 36 × 24change
Shared camera profile — set it once, every tool follows.
Formula crop = 43.267 mm / sensor diagonal · feq = f × (cropto→from)
Neq = N × ratio (depth of field & total light) · ISOeq = ISO × ratio²

Crop factor is just the ratio of two sensor diagonals. Multiply your focal length by it and you get the full-frame lens that would frame the same scene from the same spot. That part is uncontroversial, and it is where most calculators stop.

The part that starts arguments is aperture. The f-number sets image brightness on any format, so f/2.8 is f/2.8 for metering everywhere. But depth of field and the total amount of light collected by the whole frame both scale with the physical aperture diameter, and that scales with crop factor. A 25 mm f/1.8 on Micro Four Thirds frames like 50 mm and renders depth of field like f/3.6 on full frame while gathering the same total light as f/3.6 — which is exactly why the equivalent ISO scales with the square of the ratio.

Stoplume reports all three equivalences side by side and draws the two sensors to scale, so you can see the geometry instead of memorising folklore. It also states plainly what does not change: your exposure. Nothing here tells you to open up two stops for the same brightness.

Frequently asked

Does a crop sensor make my lens longer?

No. The lens projects the same image circle; the smaller sensor simply records a smaller part of it. The subject is not magnified, it is cropped — which is why perspective and compression stay exactly the same from the same shooting position.

Is f/1.8 on Micro Four Thirds 'really' f/3.6?

For brightness, no — it meters as f/1.8. For depth of field and total light gathered, yes, it behaves like f/3.6 on full frame. Both statements are true because they describe different physical quantities.

Which crop factor should I use for video?

Use the diagonal of the area actually read out. Many cameras crop further in 4K or apply a Super 35 window, so the still-photo crop factor can understate the real one. Enter the recorded sensor area as a custom size for an honest number.

Field of View & Framing Distance

Angle of view, frame size at any distance, and how far back to stand for your subject.

Full Frame 36 × 24change
Shared camera profile — set it once, every tool follows.
Formula AoV = 2 · arctan(d / 2f) · frame size = distance × sensor size / f
magnification m = f / (s − f) · required distance s = f (1 + 1/m)

Angle of view is fixed by two numbers only: the focal length and the sensor dimension it is projected onto. Everything a photographer calls 'wide' or 'long' comes out of that one arctangent. Stoplume reports horizontal, vertical and diagonal angle of view separately, because the diagonal figure quoted in lens specs is always the most flattering one and rarely the one you compose with.

The practically useful half is the inverse problem: you know the subject and the framing you want, and you need to know where to stand. Enter a subject height — 1.75 m for a standing adult, 0.30 m for a head-and-shoulders portrait, 0.05 m for a flower — and the calculator returns the exact distance at which it fills the frame, along with the reproduction ratio at that distance.

Because working distance changes perspective and focal length does not, this is also the tool that settles the 'lens compression' argument. Frame the same subject the same size from a longer lens and you must stand further back; that greater distance, not the glass, is what flattens the face and pulls the background forward.

Frequently asked

Why is the diagonal angle of view larger than the horizontal one?

Because the diagonal is the longest dimension of the frame. A 50 mm lens on full frame is about 47° diagonally but only 40° horizontally — worth remembering when a spec sheet promises a wider look than you get.

Does focal length compress perspective?

No — distance does. Perspective is set purely by where the camera stands. Long lenses are associated with compression only because they let you fill the frame from far away.

What is the ground sample distance shown in the results?

It is how many millimetres of your subject fall on a single pixel at the stated distance. It tells you whether a licence plate, a fabric weave or a bird's eye can physically be resolved before you take the shot.

Astro Shutter — NPF vs 500 Rule

Longest exposure before stars trail, computed from your pixel pitch — not a folk rule.

Full Frame 36 × 24change
Shared camera profile — set it once, every tool follows.
Type a shutter speed as 1/250, 0.5 or 30s.
Formula NPF t = k · (35 N + 30 p) / (f · cos δ) (p = pixel pitch in µm)
500 rule t = 500 / (f × crop) · drift = 15.041″/s × cos δ · arcsec per pixel = 206265 · p / (1000 f)

The 500 rule — divide 500 by your equivalent focal length — was written for 35 mm film, where grain hid a lot. On a 45 MP sensor examined at 100 % it is simply wrong, often by a factor of two, because it knows nothing about pixel pitch. The NPF rule, published by Frédéric Michaud of the Société Astronomique du Havre, fixes that by folding in aperture and the physical size of one photosite.

Stoplume computes both and shows them together so you can see the gap for your own body. It also applies the declination correction that both rules usually skip: stars near the celestial pole barely move, stars on the celestial equator move at the full 15.041 arcseconds per second, so a Polaris-side composition tolerates a much longer exposure than the same lens pointed at Orion.

Rather than asking you to trust a formula, the calculator inverts the problem. It converts your sensor and focal length into arcseconds per pixel, then reports how many pixels a star will smear across at any shutter speed you type in, and the exact exposure that keeps the smear under one pixel and under two. That is a number you can verify against your own files.

Frequently asked

Which rule should I trust?

Use NPF for anything you will print or view at 100 %, and treat the 500 rule as a rough field fallback. The two agree only by coincidence on low-resolution full-frame bodies at moderate focal lengths.

Why does declination matter?

Apparent star speed is proportional to the cosine of declination. At δ = 60° stars drift at half the equatorial rate, so you can double the exposure for the same trail length — a real, measurable gain, not a rounding trick.

Does a star tracker make this irrelevant?

It changes the question. With a tracker your limit becomes polar-alignment error and periodic error rather than Earth's rotation, but these numbers still tell you what an untracked frame in the same set can afford — useful when you shoot a tracked sky and an untracked foreground.

ND Filter & Long Exposure

Stops, optical density and the exact bulb time — in both directions.

Type a shutter speed as 1/250, 0.5 or 30s.
Type a shutter speed as 1/250, 0.5 or 30s.
Formula tND = tmetered × 2stops · optical density D = stops × 0.30103 · ND factor = 2stops
stops needed = log₂(ttarget / tmetered)

A neutral density filter does one thing: it removes a known number of stops without changing colour. Everything else is bookkeeping — and bookkeeping is exactly where long exposures go wrong, because the numbers grow as powers of two and a one-stop slip at the ten-stop end is the difference between 30 seconds and a minute.

Stoplume converts in both directions. Meter without the filter and it gives you the bulb time formatted in hours, minutes and seconds. Or state the exposure time you want — two seconds to smooth water, thirty to erase a crowd, four minutes to turn clouds into streaks — and it tells you exactly how many stops of filtration that requires and which stack of common filters gets you there.

The three naming systems are shown together because manufacturers refuse to agree: the same glass is an ND1000, a 3.0 and a ten-stop depending on the box. Stoplume prints the filter factor, the optical density and the stop count for every result so you can match whatever is engraved on your own filter ring.

Frequently asked

Do I need to compensate for reciprocity failure?

On digital sensors, no — silicon response stays linear into multi-minute exposures. It matters on film, where exposures beyond roughly one second need extra time according to the film's own published curve. Stoplume computes the digital case.

Why does my ten-stop filter give a colour cast?

Because 'neutral' is an aspiration. Dense resin and cheaper glass filters often transmit unevenly across the spectrum and infrared leaks past the visible band. Shoot raw, set a custom white balance through the filter, and the cast is recoverable.

Can I stack filters instead of buying a stronger one?

Yes, stops add linearly: a 6-stop plus a 3-stop is a 9-stop. The costs are extra reflections, more vignetting on wide lenses, and compounded colour casts. Stoplume's stacking field adds the extra stops for you.

Flash Guide Number & Inverse Square

Aperture from guide number, and how fast light dies as your subject steps back.

Formula GN = N × d (at ISO 100) · N = GN √(ISO/100) √power / d
Illuminance E ∝ 1 / d² · stop difference = 2 · log₂(d₂ / d₁)

Flash exposure obeys one equation with three terms. The guide number is the manufacturer's shorthand for how much light the head puts out at ISO 100: divide it by the flash-to-subject distance and you have the aperture that will expose correctly. Rearranged, the same relation tells you how far to place the light or what guide number a scene demands.

The inverse square law is the part that surprises people on set. Light does not fade gently — brightness falls with the square of distance, so a subject at 4.5 m from a light that correctly exposes at 3 m is more than a stop dark. Stoplume prints a falloff table and draws the curve, and computes the lighting ratio between two subjects standing at different distances from the same head.

That curve is also a creative tool. Close to the light, the falloff between a subject's nose and their shoulder is steep, so the background goes black; move the light back and the same scene turns even and flat. Knowing where you sit on the curve is the difference between guessing at test frames and placing a light on purpose.

Frequently asked

Does shutter speed affect flash exposure?

Not for the flash itself, as long as you stay at or below sync speed — the burst is far shorter than the exposure. Shutter speed controls the ambient light only, which is precisely how you balance flash against daylight.

Why is my real-world exposure darker than the guide number predicts?

Guide numbers are quoted at a specific zoom head position, usually in a small reflective room, and often optimistically. Any modifier costs light: a shoot-through umbrella typically eats one to two stops. Treat the result as a starting point and meter.

Does the inverse square law apply to softboxes?

It applies exactly to point sources and approximately to any source once you are several source-widths away. Very close to a large softbox the falloff is gentler than 1/d², which is why big modifiers used close give such even light across a group.

Golden Hour & Sun Position

Sunrise, sunset, golden and blue hour for any place and date — computed offline.

Formula NOAA solar position algorithm — Julian day → geometric mean longitude → equation of centre → apparent longitude → declination and equation of time → hour angle at target elevation.
Golden hour: sun elevation −4° … +6° · Blue hour: −6° … −4° · Civil twilight: −6°

Warm light near the horizon is not a mood, it is atmospheric physics. When the sun is low its rays travel through many times more air than at noon; short wavelengths scatter away and what reaches your subject is the long, warm remainder, arriving almost horizontally. That is why golden hour flatters faces and rakes across texture — and why it is short, and why its length depends enormously on your latitude and the date.

Stoplume implements the NOAA solar position algorithm directly in your browser. Nothing is sent to a server, and the calculation works with no network at all once the page is loaded. Enter coordinates and a date and you get sunrise, sunset, solar noon, civil twilight, the morning and evening golden hour windows, the blue hour, total day length, and the compass azimuth the sun rises and sets at — which is the number that actually decides whether the light will hit the front of your building.

The elevation thresholds are stated rather than hidden: golden hour is taken as the sun between −4° and +6°, blue hour between −6° and −4°, civil twilight at −6°, and sunrise/sunset at −0.833° to account for refraction and the solar disk radius. Different apps choose slightly different cut-offs, so the definition matters more than another decimal place.

Frequently asked

Why do golden hour times differ between apps?

Because 'golden hour' has no standards body. Some apps use 0° to +6°, others −4° to +6°. Stoplume states its thresholds on the page, so you can compare like with like instead of assuming one app is broken.

How accurate is the calculation?

The NOAA algorithm it implements is accurate to well under a minute for dates between 1901 and 2099 at latitudes below about 72°. Near the poles, and on days when the sun barely crosses a threshold, precision degrades and Stoplume says so instead of printing a false time.

What is the azimuth good for?

Planning. If you know sunrise is at azimuth 62° you can check on any map whether the light will reach a west-facing valley or be blocked by a ridge — before driving there at four in the morning.

Time-lapse Interval Planner

Interval, clip length, frame count, card space and motion blur — solve for any one.

Type a shutter speed as 1/250, 0.5 or 30s.
Formula frames = shooting time / interval · clip length = frames / fps · speed-up = interval × fps
shutter angle equivalent = 360° × shutter / interval · storage = frames × file size

A time-lapse is a resampling problem. You capture the world at one frame every few seconds and replay it at twenty-four or thirty, so the multiplication factor — interval times playback frame rate — is how much faster than life the finished clip runs. Everything else in the plan follows from that one product.

The trap is arithmetic done in the field at 5 a.m. Twenty seconds of 24 fps output needs 480 frames; at a five-second interval that is forty minutes of standing there, and at 30 MB a frame it is over fourteen gigabytes. Stoplume solves in whichever direction you need — set the clip you want and get the interval, or set the interval and see the clip — and reports frames, card space and battery-relevant duration together.

The number most planners omit is shutter angle. Motion in a time-lapse looks smooth when each frame is exposed for a decent fraction of its interval, and stutters when it is not. Stoplume converts your shutter and interval into the cine-equivalent shutter angle, so you can aim for the 180° look deliberately instead of discovering strobing after the render.

Frequently asked

What interval should I use?

It depends on how fast the subject moves in frame, not on the subject's name. Fast clouds and traffic want 1–2 s, drifting clouds and shadows 3–8 s, sunsets 4–6 s, stars 20–30 s, construction sites minutes. Aim for a small, consistent change between neighbouring frames.

What does the shutter angle number mean?

It is the cine equivalent of your exposure fraction: 180° means the shutter was open for half the interval, the classic smooth-motion look. Below about 90° motion starts to stutter; above 270° it smears. Add an ND filter to lengthen the shutter without overexposing.

Should I shoot raw or JPEG?

Raw if you will grade or do a holy-grail day-to-night ramp, because you will need the highlight and white-balance latitude. JPEG if the card, the battery or the shutter count is the binding constraint. The storage figure here updates with whichever file size you enter.

Macro Magnification & Effective Aperture

Extension tubes, reproduction ratio, bellows compensation and the depth of field you actually get.

Full Frame 36 × 24change
Shared camera profile — set it once, every tool follows.
Formula mtotal = mlens + extension / f · Neff = N (1 + m / p)
exposure compensation = 2 log₂(1 + m / p) stops · DoF ≈ 2 N c (1 + m/p) / m²

At normal distances a lens behaves like a simple projector and the f-number printed on the barrel is the aperture the light actually sees. In macro that stops being true. As you rack the lens forward the exit pupil moves further from the sensor, the cone of light spreads over a longer throw, and the effective aperture becomes N(1 + m/p) — at 1:1 with a symmetric lens, a marked f/8 is really f/16.

Two consequences follow immediately. First, you lose light: two stops at 1:1, which is why a metered flash setting or an ambient reading taken at infinity comes out dark. Cameras metering through the lens absorb this automatically, but manual flash calculations do not, so Stoplume prints the bellows compensation in stops.

Second, diffraction arrives far earlier than you expect, because the Airy disk is set by the effective aperture, not the marked one. Macro depth of field is measured in millimetres and the temptation is to stop down to f/22 — where an effective f/44 has already destroyed more detail than the extra depth recovers. Stoplume shows magnification, working distance, the subject area covered by your sensor, effective aperture, exposure compensation and depth of field at once, so the trade-off is visible before you commit to a focus stack.

Frequently asked

How much magnification does an extension tube add?

Extension divided by focal length. A 25 mm tube on a 50 mm lens adds 0.5×; the same tube on a 200 mm lens adds only 0.125×. Tubes are therefore far more effective on short lenses, at the cost of a very short working distance.

What is pupil magnification and where do I find it?

It is the ratio of exit to entrance pupil diameter. Symmetric lenses are near 1, telephoto designs are below 1 and retrofocus wide-angles above 1. Most manufacturers do not publish it; leaving it at 1 is the standard assumption and is accurate for typical macro primes.

Why is my macro shot soft even though focus looks right?

Usually effective-aperture diffraction, subject motion, or depth of field thinner than the subject. Check the effective aperture here first: if it is past f/16 equivalent on your format, open up a stop and stack frames instead.
